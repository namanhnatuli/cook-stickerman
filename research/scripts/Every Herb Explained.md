Herbs do not all work the same way. Some fade under heat, some get stronger, and some only work dried. Once you understand that, everything else makes sense. Basil, fresh basil and dried basil are so different, they are almost two separate ingredients. The fresh leaves carry a bright, slightly sweet flavor with a faint anis edge that starts fading the moment the leaf is cut, which is why it goes on pizza after it comes out of the oven, torn into pasta at the end, or blended into pesto. Cooking it dulls the very thing you are using it for. One is about freshness. Dried basil works completely differently. It goes in at the beginning of long cooked tomato sauces and brazes where heat has time to draw out what is left of its flavor. The other is about background depth. Cilantro. No herb splits people more completely. The reason is not preference. It is genetics. A specific variation in smell receptors makes cilantro taste unmistakably of soap to a significant portion of the population regardless of how it is prepared. For everyone else, it is bright, citrusy, and irreplaceable in Mexican, Thai, and Indian cooking. Compared to parsley, which is clean and neutral, cilantro is sharper and more assertive. The two look almost identical, but they behave completely differently in a dish. The stems carry the same flavor, but they hold up better under heat, so they go into the dish during cooking, while the leaves stay for the finish. Rosemary. One of the few herbs that get stronger rather than weaker under heat. The needle-like leaves are tough enough to survive long roasting without breaking down, releasing their oils slowly into whatever surrounds them, which is why it works so well with lamb, pork, and potatoes, where fat carries that flavor through the entire dish. Where time builds quietly in the background, rosemary announces itself immediately. That intensity is also the risk. too much and it takes over. Strip the leaves from the stem and use the bare woody stem as a skewer for grilling. It releases flavor directly into the meat as it cooks in a way that seasoning on the outside cannot replicate. Parsley, the herb that makes heavy food taste less heavy. Flat leaf parsley has a clean, slightly peppery brightness that cuts through rich dishes like steak, roasted meat, and pasta without taking over. Most people use only the leaves and throw away the stems, but the stems carry more flavor than anything above them. Chop them finely and they disappear into the dish while adding depth the leaves alone don't produce. Curly parsley is milder and more decorative, but flat leaf is the better choice when you actually want parsley flavor in the food rather than on top of it. Mint. Mint feels cold even when it isn't. Menthol triggers cold receptors in the mouth, which is why it creates that cooling effect without actually changing the temperature. That is also why it works so well in drinks, desserts, and certain savory dishes where freshness matters more than depth. In the kitchen, two types matter. Spearmint is softer and slightly sweet, the one used in most cooking. Peppermint is sharper and more intense, which is why it leans more toward chocolate and confectionary than savory food. Mint does not handle heat well. Its brightest aroma fades quickly, so it goes in at the very last moment because heat dulls it fast. Thyme is the herb you don't notice until it's missing. The flavor is earthy, slightly floral, and built for time. Unlike softer herbs, it holds up under long cooking, slowly working its way into stocks, brazes, and roasts. That's what it does. It makes food taste like it has been cooking longer than it has. Where rosemary is loud and immediate, time builds quietly in the background without ever announcing itself. Strip the leaves by running your fingers backward along the stem. Then, drop both leaves and stem into the pot. The flavor develops slowly and the stem comes out before serving. Oregano, one of the few herbs that is actually more useful dried than fresh. The opposite of basil. The fresh leaves are mild and slightly bitter, but not very expressive. Drying concentrates the essential oils and amplifies the flavor into something far more assertive, producing the sharp, slightly peppery warmth that defines pizza, Greek salad, and tomato sauce. That intensity means a little goes a long way. And unlike most dried herbs, the flavor holds for months without fading significantly. Chives, the mildest member of the onion family with a clean, delicate flavor that sits somewhere between onion and garlic without the sharpness of either. Because the flavor is so subtle, chives work almost exclusively as a finishing herb scattered over eggs, stirred into sour cream, or laid across a steak just before serving. Cooking dulls them almost completely. They need no heat, no time, just a sharp knife and a light hand. The hollow stems bruise easily, which is why they should always be cut rather than torn [music] and always added at the very last moment. Chives are not for cooking. They're for finishing. Dill. It has a very specific flavor that does not blend into a dish. It sits on top and announces itself immediately. [music] Fresh, slightly anislike, with a clean sharpness that works naturally with fish, cucumber, and dairy. That directness is why it is so closely associated with specific dishes like smoked salmon, satziki, and pickles. [snorts] Rather than used as a background herb the way thyme or parsley would be where teragon has a similar anis edge but is sweeter and more elegant, dill is sharper and more immediate. [music] Added at the end or not at all. Heat dulls it fast. sage, dense, slightly furry leaves with a flavor that is earthy and strong enough to stand up to the richest ingredients in the kitchen. It cuts through fat rather than getting lost in it, which is why it works so well with pork, butter, and aged cheeses. Fry whole leaves and butter until they crisp at the edges, and the butter turns golden and nutty underneath. What was sharp becomes deeper and more complex. It is one of the few herbs that actually improves under direct heat rather than fading from it. Use too much and the flavor turns medicinal. Get the amount right and nothing else does quite what sage does. Peragon is the defining herb in Bernese sauce. Anyone who has eaten steak with bones has tasted teragon without necessarily knowing its name. The flavor is distinctly anislike, sweeter, and more elegant than dill without the sharpness. It is one of the four classic French fines herbs, and it shows up throughout French cooking in ways that feel subtle until you notice it is missing. Fresh teragon is significantly more complex than dried. Dried teragon loses most of the delicate top notes and leaves something flat. If you have ever wondered what gives a good bones that particular depth, this is the answer. Bay leaf. The most debated herb in the kitchen. Many home cooks have wondered at some point whether it actually does anything. It does, but not in the way most herbs work. Bay leaf does not add a flavor you can point to directly. What it does is round out the other flavors around it, adding a subtle herbal depth that makes a stock or braze taste more complete without ever tasting of bay leaf itself. Remove it early and something feels missing. Always remove it before serving. The leaf itself is too tough to eat and the flavor turns bitter if it sits too long. Lemongrass. It does not look like a herb and it does not behave like one. The long pale stocks are too woody and fibrous to eat directly, so they go into a dish whole or bruised to release flavor, then are removed before serving. What they release is a clean, intensely citrusy aroma that is completely different from lemon or lime. There is no acidity, just fragrance that lifts the entire dish without adding sourness. It is the backbone of Thai curry pastes and Vietnamese broths. And the reason certain Asian dishes have that distinctive aromatic quality that is difficult to replicate with anything else. Lovage. It tastes like celery turned all the way up. Deeper, more savory, slightly bitter, and strong enough that a single stem can replace an entire bunch of celery in a stock or braze. That's where it works best. Use it in slowcooked dishes that need a backbone you do not necessarily notice, but would miss if it were not there. Lovage is almost absent from modern supermarkets, which is why most people have never tasted it, even though the flavor feels instantly familiar. That's every herb explained.
